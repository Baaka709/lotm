At 2 Daffodil Street, in a living room washed in the amber glow of dusk, Klein stood before the oriel window and relayed his divination to Frye and Leonard. He had seen a grayish-blue two-story building in his dream—first-floor windows shuttered, curtains drawn, surrounded by bare brown soil and a desolate, horror-story garden. The only identifying landmark was a nearby river, broad enough to be either the Tussock or the Khoy. Process of elimination, Klein told them, was their only option.

Leonard, leaning back with his characteristic ease, suggested they could narrow it down further. A careful criminal, he reasoned, would naturally place his altar far from his hunting grounds, only venturing closer when distant targets ran dry. They should look for whichever area showed the sharpest rise in unexplained deaths—that region would be nearest the altar and therefore off-limits to the killer's victims. Klein's eyes lit up at the insight, privately lamenting that detective instinct was simply not in his blood. Frye quietly rifled through the stack of documents on the coffee table, and after a few minutes confirmed it: there was exactly one such area. The West Borough.

They narrowed the search to the southwest corner, close to where the Tussock River edged the borough, and hired a two-wheeled carriage. As it rolled along the muddy road, the dying sun set the broad river ablaze in red and orange. Klein and Frye pressed their faces to opposite windows, scanning each building. Leonard sat back and hummed a tune. Then, at the edge of his vision, Klein caught it—a grayish-blue two-story structure, its garden a ruin of gloom, its first-floor curtains pulled tight against the world. He barely kept his voice steady as he said they had found it. The carriage rolled on past without stopping, carrying them out of sight before Leonard signalled the driver to halt.

Leonard looked at Klein and, with a smirk that carried more warmth than it let on, told him to take the carriage back to Zouteland Street and fetch the Captain. Frye agreed. Klein understood—his combat training had only just begun, and whoever had committed so many murders for a ritual would not be a simple opponent. He looked at both of them, managed a thin smile, and told them to be careful. Leonard waved it off cheerfully, promising they would only keep watch until Dunn arrived. Before Klein stepped away, he produced a copper penny and divined for them. The coin landed on the King's head. He cautioned them, as a Seer should, that the symbol remained blurry and open to interpretation—prudence above all.

Leonard was already jumping from the carriage, calling back that Klein fussed like an eighty-year-old grandmother. Frye descended in silence, suitcase in hand. Klein watched them disappear into the dusk and directed the driver to Zouteland Street.

The Blackthorn Security Company was dim and deserted when he arrived, the others long gone for the day. Dunn sat motionless on the guest sofa in his black windbreaker, nearly invisible in the unlit room, and his voice rising from the dark gave Klein a start. Klein briefed him quickly—Leonard's hypothesis, the divination, the discovered house. Dunn listened, cutting in only occasionally, then rose and walked straight for the door. Halfway down the stairs he paused and told Klein to stay behind in case of emergencies at the office. Then, almost as an afterthought, he reversed himself: lock up and follow, but stay fifty meters back at all times and come no closer until it was over. Klein could help with ritualistic matters during the final inspection if needed, and the experience would do him good. Klein nodded firmly and said he understood.

Night had fully settled by the time they reached the scene. The Tussock River moved dark and restless beside the road, and the grayish-blue building crouched in the shadows like something alive, its ruined garden utterly silent—no insects, no rustling, nothing. Klein stood at a distance, palms damp, body trembling, with the oppressive sense that something hungry and invisible was coiled within that stillness. He watched Dunn, Leonard, and Frye dissolve into the darkness as they advanced on the building.

Inside, on the unlit second floor, a gentle-faced young woman named Trissy sat finishing her skincare routine before her dressing table. A silver mirror lay beside her, its surface so coarsely ground it could barely hold a reflection. Then blood began to seep from it. Trissy's soft expression went cold and still. She rose, moved to the window, and looked out into the night without a word.
